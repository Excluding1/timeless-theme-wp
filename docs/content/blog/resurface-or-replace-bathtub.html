The short answer

If your bath is structurally sound and the damage is cosmetic, chips, stains, worn enamel or a peeling old coating, resurfacing is almost always the better call. It takes about a day and costs a fraction of replacement. Replacement only makes sense when the tub is cracked right through, rusted out underneath, or you are relocating plumbing anyway.

That is the honest version, and the rest of this guide explains how we actually make that call on real Sydney bathrooms, including the jobs where we tell people not to resurface.

What bath resurfacing actually involves

Bath resurfacing (you will also hear re-enamelling, reglazing or refinishing, they all describe the same job) is the process of restoring your existing bath rather than ripping it out. The bath stays exactly where it is. No demolition, no plumber, no tiler.

A proper job looks like this:

  1. Repair. Chips, scratches and worn patches are filled and sanded back so the surface is sound.
  2. Preparation. The old surface is cleaned, degreased and abraded, and a bonding agent is applied. This step is most of the work and it is the step that decides how long the finish lasts.
  3. Masking and ventilation. The room is masked up and extraction fans manage fumes.
  4. Coating. A professional two-part coating is sprayed on in multiple layers for a smooth, glossy, factory-style finish.
  5. Curing. The bath is left to cure before use, usually around 24 hours. We confirm the exact cure time on the day.

The result is a bath that looks and feels new, in the same spot, with the same plumbing, for far less than the cost of pulling it out. You can read more about the process on our bath resurfacing service page.

One thing that surprises people: resurfacing is also how you change a bath's colour. The pink, mission-brown and avocado tubs of the seventies and eighties are still solid baths under the surface, and a resurface in modern white transforms the whole room without a renovation. If your bath's only crime is its era, that is a resurfacing job too.

When resurfacing is the right call

In our experience, most tired baths in Sydney homes are candidates for resurfacing. The tub itself is usually fine. It is the surface that has given up. Resurfacing is the right choice when:

  • The damage is cosmetic. Stains, dullness, scratches, worn enamel, surface rust marks or a handful of chips. A chip on its own may not even need a full resurface, see our chipped bathtub repair service.
  • The bath is solid. No flex underfoot, no cracks that go through the material, no soft spots in an acrylic tub.
  • You have a cast-iron or pressed-steel original. Older Sydney homes often have heavy cast-iron baths that hold heat beautifully and simply cannot be bought like-for-like today. Resurfacing keeps them.
  • The bathroom around it is staying. If the tiles, waterproofing and layout are staying put, pulling the bath out creates damage you then have to pay to repair.
  • You need the bathroom back quickly. Rental turnarounds, pre-sale tidy-ups, or simply a family with one bathroom.

When replacement is genuinely better

We would rather tell you this before you spend money with us. Some baths should not be resurfaced, and a coating will not fix a structural problem. Replacement is the honest answer when:

  • The bath is cracked through. A hairline surface crack can often be repaired, but a crack that flexes or lets water through the shell is structural. A coating over the top will fail.
  • Steel is rusted from underneath. If rust has eaten through from the underside of a pressed-steel bath, the metal itself is failing. Surface treatment cannot reverse that.
  • An acrylic tub has lost its support. If the base flexes badly when you stand in it, the problem is under the bath, not on it.
  • You are changing the bathroom anyway. Moving the bath, changing the layout, or doing a full renovation with new waterproofing. If the room is being gutted, replace the bath while the walls are open.
  • A previous owner has resurfaced it badly more than once. Multiple failed coatings can sometimes be stripped and redone, but occasionally the honest advice is to stop spending money on that tub.

Time and disruption compared

This is the part most people underestimate. Replacing a bath is never just replacing a bath.

To get an old tub out, the tiles around it usually have to come off. That breaks the waterproofing membrane, which by law and by common sense then needs to be redone. So a straightforward bath swap becomes demolition, waterproofing, tiling, plumbing and days of trades traffic through your house. One to two weeks without a bathroom is a normal outcome, and longer if trades are booked out.

The bath that comes out in a demolition is rarely the only thing that gets replaced. The tiles, the waterproofing and half the wall usually follow it out the door.

Resurfacing, by comparison, is about a day of on-site work. One person, one van, extraction fans running, and the bath is back in service after curing, usually around 24 hours later. No skip bin on the driveway, no dust through the house.

How long a professional resurface lasts

A professionally applied two-part coating, on a properly prepared bath, typically lasts 10 to 15 years with sensible care. Sensible care means non-abrasive cleaners, no suction mats left on the surface, and not letting taps drip on the same spot for years.

Our resurfacing work is backed by a warranty of up to 5 years, provided the surface is looked after as set out in our care instructions. That is the warranty, not the lifespan. A well-kept resurface should comfortably outlive it. We wrote a full guide on this, how long bath resurfacing lasts and what makes it fail early, if you want the detail.

How to decide in 5 minutes

Run through this checklist with your own bath. It is the same logic we apply when we quote.

  1. Press on the base and sides. Solid, no flex, no soft spots? Point to resurfacing.
  2. Look for cracks. Surface crazing and hairlines are fixable. A crack you can catch a fingernail in, that flexes or weeps water, points to replacement.
  3. Check underneath if you can. Rust stains bleeding from below a steel bath are a warning sign. Surface rust around the plughole is usually repairable.
  4. Ask what else is changing. Keeping the bathroom as it is? Resurface. Gutting the room anyway? Replace while the walls are open.
  5. Count the bathrooms. If this is your only one, the difference between one day and two weeks without it may make the decision for you.

Frequently asked questions

Is it cheaper to resurface or replace a bath?

Resurfacing is significantly cheaper, typically a fraction of the cost of replacement. Replacing a bath is rarely just the tub. It usually means demolition, new waterproofing, tiling and plumbing on top of the bath itself. Resurfacing is one trade and about one day of work, with none of those extra costs.

How long does a resurfaced bath last?

A professionally resurfaced bath typically lasts 10 to 15 years with proper care. That means non-abrasive cleaners, no suction mats left on the surface, and fixing dripping taps. Our resurfacing work is backed by a warranty of up to 5 years when the surface is cared for per our care instructions.

Can a cast-iron bath be resurfaced?

Yes, cast-iron baths are excellent candidates for resurfacing. They are structurally very strong, hold heat well, and genuine like-for-like replacements are hard to find and heavy to install. Resurfacing restores the enamelled surface while keeping the original tub, which is why so many owners of older Sydney homes choose it.

Can you resurface a bath twice?

Yes, a bath can be resurfaced again, including over a failed earlier coating. The key is that the old coating must be properly stripped or fully abraded back first, not just sprayed over. Done correctly, a second resurface performs like the first. We assess the existing coating as part of the quote.

How soon can I use the bath afterwards?

Most resurfaced baths are ready for normal use after about 24 hours of curing. We confirm the exact cure time for your job on the day, since products and conditions vary. Holding off for the full cure period matters, as early water contact is one of the main causes of premature coating failure.
