Tile paint is one of the most searched bathroom fixes in Australia, and for good reason: it is cheap, it is a weekend, and the before-and-after photos look excellent. The honest answer to whether it works is that it depends almost entirely on where you put it, and the place most people want to put it is the worst place for it.

The short answer

Yes, you can paint bathroom tiles. On a dry wall away from water it often holds up fine. In a shower recess or above a bath it typically starts lifting within a year or two, and it fails in flakes rather than fading gracefully. Removing failed paint is then harder than the original job.

Why tile paint fails in a wet area

A glazed tile is designed to shed water and reject anything trying to stick to it. That is the entire point of a glaze. Paint applied over it sits on the surface rather than bonding into it. It is held on by whatever mechanical grip the primer can find, which in a wet area is not much, and not for long.

Three things then work against it at once. Steam and standing water get behind the film at every edge and grout line. The tile expands and contracts with hot water while the coating does not, at a slightly different rate. And every clean, especially with anything abrasive or bleach-based, takes a little more off.

The part that costs money later

Failed tile paint is not a neutral outcome, and this is the part the product boxes leave out. You do not return to where you started. You arrive somewhere worse. There is now a partially bonded film across every tile and grout line, and it has to come off before anything proper can go on.

Stripping it is slow, messy work, and it is charged as work. A wall that could have been resurfaced or tiled over cleanly the first time now carries a preparation cost that did not exist before the weekend project. It is the single most common reason a job we quote costs more than the same job would have a year earlier.

What professional resurfacing does differently

The visible part of both jobs is a coating going onto a tile. The difference is entirely in what happens before that, and in what the coating actually is. Preparation is most of the labour on a professional job and almost none of it on a DIY one, which is exactly why the outcomes diverge at the twelve-month mark.

Here is the part worth being precise about, because it is not the part people expect. Both systems sand. White Knight's own instructions for their tile primer tell you to clean the surface with their cleaner, "lightly sand using #240 grit wet & dry sandpaper", rinse and dry it. That is real preparation and it is not the difference.

The difference is what goes on afterwards. A consumer system puts one coat of consumer primer on a scuffed glaze and then a single-pack topcoat that dries by evaporation, applied with a brush or a roller. What we put on is a chemical bonding step that keys into the glaze itself, then a catalysed primer, then a two-pack topcoat that cures by reaction rather than by drying, sprayed in multiple coats rather than brushed. It ends up part of the surface rather than a skin resting on it.

The bonding agent we use on wall tile is the part you cannot replicate, and not because it is a secret. It is a trade product from an exclusive distributor, not a hardware-store line, and it sits in a chemical range restricted enough that some of the products in it ship as class 8 dangerous goods and are not sold to a working tradesperson at all until they hold a training sign-off covering safe handling and breathing gear.

That is the shape of the gap. Not a better brand of paint, but a different class of product with a different set of rules attached to it, laid down by people who spray these surfaces for a living.

Walls yes, floors no: the rule we work to

This is where we will talk you out of something we sell. Wall tiles and floor tiles are not the same problem, and treating them the same is why plenty of coated bathrooms look poor within a year even when the work was done properly.

Wall tiles are a good candidate for resurfacing. Nobody walks on them, so the coating is never asked to survive a point load, and a wall carries fewer grout lines per square metre than a floor of the same area.

Worth noticing: the tile paints themselves are sold for walls. White Knight, the most widely stocked tile paint in Australia, describes its RENEW Tile & Laminate Paint as being for ceramic wall tiles, laminate and melamine. Floors are not mentioned on the product page at all, in either direction.

That absence is the useful part. A manufacturer who believed the product performed on floors would say so, because it would sell more paint. If the product you are holding does not claim floors, take the silence seriously.

Floor tiles are different, and small formats especially. A small tile means far more grout lines per square metre. A floor is stepped on constantly, and those lines are exactly where the movement concentrates. A coating spanning a grid that is already moving will crack along it. On floors, tiling over the existing surface gives you a new continuous floor rather than a film sitting on top of an old one, and it lasts.

Does painting tiles affect the waterproofing?

No, and this is the one worry we can put straight to bed. Bathroom waterproofing is a membrane that sits under the tile bed, not the tile face. Painting or coating the visible surface does not touch it, so a tile paint job cannot undo the waterproofing of a bathroom that was built correctly.

That is the Code's own position. The National Construction Code's housing provisions require that "building elements in wet areas within a building must be protected with a waterproofing system", and where a screed is used that membrane can sit above or below the tile bed or screed. What the provisions never do is treat a decorative coating on the face of a tile as part of the waterproofing system, in either direction.

So nobody is stopping you. The reasons not to paint a shower are practical, not regulatory, and the reason we ask about the grout before quoting is the same: grout and silicone are what usually let water past, and a coating over failing joints hides the problem without fixing it.

There is one thing worth knowing before coating a floor, though. Many bathroom floor tiles are sold with a slip rating, tested wet, under AS 4586. That rating belongs to the tile that was tested. Put a coating over it and the tested surface is no longer the surface underfoot, and no rating covers what you have now. It is not a rule anyone enforces in a private home. It is just a thing worth knowing before coating the one floor in the house that is routinely wet and walked on barefoot.

What to do if you have already painted them

Nothing urgent, and nothing to feel silly about. Plenty of people try it, including people who did careful preparation. What matters now is not making the next decision worse, and the worst next move is another coat of the same thing over the top of a film that is already letting go.

One note on cover while we are here, because this article is about the wall-versus-floor split and the warranty follows that same split. Wall-tile resurfacing carries up to 5 years of workmanship cover in an owner-occupied home. Common questions about painting bathroom tiles

Can you paint tiles in a shower?

You can, and it is the place tile paint fails fastest. A shower recess is wet for hours a day, gets steam behind the film at every grout line, and is cleaned more aggressively than any other surface in the house. If you are painting anywhere, a dry wall away from direct water gives you a far better chance.

How long does tile paint last in a bathroom?

On a dry wall, several years is realistic. In a shower or above a bath, one to two years is more common before the first edges lift. The failure is rarely gradual: it holds up, then water gets behind one corner and the coating starts coming away in sheets rather than wearing thin.

Is professional tile resurfacing just better paint?

No, and not in the way people assume. Both systems sand the glaze first, so that is not the difference. Ours adds a chemical bonding step into the glaze, a catalysed primer and a two-pack topcoat that cures by reaction, all sprayed rather than brushed. The kit scuffs, primes once and rolls on a coat that dries by evaporation.

Can I paint over tile paint that is already peeling?

It will look fine briefly and fail faster than the first attempt. The new coat can only be as sound as what is under it, and what is under it is already letting go. The film has to come off before anything proper goes on, which is the cost that makes a second attempt more expensive than the first.

Should I paint or resurface my bathroom floor tiles?

Neither, in most cases. A floor is walked on constantly and carries far more grout lines per square metre than a wall, so a coating cracks along those lines. Tiling over the existing floor gives you a new continuous surface rather than a film over a moving grid, and it holds up far better.
