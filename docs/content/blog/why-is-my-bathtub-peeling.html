The short answer

If your bathtub is peeling, a previous coating is failing. Factory enamel does not peel, it wears and stains, but only an applied coating can lift off in flakes or sheets. The usual culprit is a DIY paint kit or a cheap earlier resurface. The fix is to strip the failed layer and resurface properly, not to paint over it.

That one fact, enamel does not peel, coatings do, tells you most of what you need to know. It means your bath has been coated at some point, even if that was before you owned it, and it means the problem is fixable.

DIY kit vs professional coating: why the difference shows at 12 months

Around the one-year mark is when we get the calls. The bath was painted, it looked great for a few months, and now it is shedding. The reason people ask why is my bathtub peeling so often is that the two ways of coating a bath look identical on day one and behave nothing alike by month twelve.

Here is the part that surprises people, and it is worth stating plainly because the easy version of this story is wrong. A hardware-store bath kit is not cheap one-part paint. White Knight's tub and basin product is a two-pack epoxy enamel system, the same broad chemistry family we work with. The tin is not where these two jobs separate.

They separate on everything around the tin. A kit goes on by roller or brush, so film thickness is whatever your hand leaves, and thin patches are where lifting starts. The prep is whatever the person doing it has the patience for. There is no sheeting, no masking, no forced ventilation, and no trade bonding primer under it.

A professional resurface is a different job wearing the same name. The surface is chemically degreased, repaired, mechanically abraded and primed with a bonding agent before a two-part coating is sprayed on in controlled layers. The materials cost more, the preparation takes hours, and the result bonds to the bath instead of sitting on it.

A coating does not decide how long it lasts. The preparation underneath it already made that decision.

The kit makers say the same thing, which is the part worth noticing. The instructions on a common hardware-store tub and basin kit require you to sand the entire surface with 240 to 320 grit to remove all gloss, clean off soap deposits and body fats with an abrasive powder, and then leave it a minimum of five days before anyone uses the bath. Most failed DIY jobs skipped at least one of those.

Other causes of a peeling bathtub: cleaners, mats and trapped moisture

Preparation failures cause most peeling, but they are not the only route to it. A coating that went on properly can still be destroyed early by what happens afterwards. Three things do most of that damage, and all three are avoidable once you know about them.

They are worth reading even if your bath is fine today, because every one of them is doing its damage quietly for months before anything visible happens.

  • Harsh and abrasive cleaners. Scourers, cream cleansers and strong solvent products thin and dull a coating until edges start to lift. On a coated bath, mild detergent and a soft cloth are all you need.
  • Suction mats left in place. They trap water against the surface for days at a time, and ripping them off pulls at the coating. If you use one, lift it out after each bath.
  • Trapped moisture. If the bath was coated while damp, or water is getting under the edge from a failed silicone bead or a dripping tap, it lifts the coating from below. This is why peeling so often starts at the plughole, the tap end, or the silicone line.
  • Using the bath before the coating cured. The first 48 to 72 hours decide a lot. Water in the bath before then weakens the bond permanently.

Sydney makes trapped moisture worse than it sounds. We pulled the Bureau of Meteorology's long-run record for Observatory Hill and counted it ourselves: mean 9am relative humidity sits at or above 70% for seven months of the year and averages 69% annually. A bathroom here does not dry out between uses the way one in a drier climate does, so anything holding water against the surface holds it there for longer.

Why you cannot just paint over it

It is the obvious-looking shortcut, and it fails every time. Whatever you apply over a peeling coating is only ever as attached as the loose layer underneath it. The old coating keeps letting go on its own schedule, and now it takes your new paint with it, usually within months.

Painting over also seals the original problem in. If moisture or contamination caused the first failure, it is still under there, working on layer two. Every extra coat makes the eventual proper fix slower and more expensive, because all of it has to come off. If you take one thing from this article: do not put another coat on a peeling bath.

The proper fix: strip, prep, resurface

The right repair removes the failure rather than hiding it, which is why a second coat over a peeling first coat is not a repair at all. Everything loose has to come off, the surface underneath has to be made ready to bond to, and only then does anything new go on. Three stages, in this order:

How to keep it from happening again

Once your bath has been resurfaced properly, keeping it that way is genuinely easy, and none of it is a product you have to buy from us. The finish is hard but it is a coating, not enamel, so it dislikes the same three things every time: abrasives, anything trapped against it, and heavy chemicals sitting on the surface:

  • No water or use until the cure time we give you has passed.
  • Clean with mild detergent and a soft cloth. No scourers, no abrasive creams.
  • Do not leave suction mats in the bath between uses.
  • Fix dripping taps early. A steady drip on one spot wears any surface.
  • If the silicone around the bath fails, replace it promptly so water cannot creep under the coating edge.

That short list is the difference between a coating that fails early and one that runs well past its warranty. It is the same care routine that keeps our up-to-5-year resurfacing warranty intact.

Frequently asked questions

Can I paint over a peeling bathtub?

No, and it will cost you more in the end. New paint bonds to the loose coating underneath, so it peels on the same schedule, usually within months. It also seals in whatever caused the first failure. The failed coating has to be stripped back to a sound surface before anything new goes on.

Is a peeling bath dangerous?

It is not toxic to be around, but it is worth fixing promptly. Flaking edges can be sharp underfoot, loose flakes end up in the drain, and the exposed patches let water reach the bath material underneath, which causes rust in steel tubs and staining in others. The longer it runs, the bigger the repair.

Can a peeling bath be resurfaced again?

Yes, in almost every case. The failed coating is stripped or fully abraded off, the damage underneath is repaired, and the bath is properly prepared and resurfaced with a professional two-part coating. Done that way, it performs like a first-time resurface, covered by our warranty of up to 5 years with proper care.

How long does the proper fix take?

About one day on site for a typical bath, including stripping the failed coating, repairs, preparation and spraying. The bath then needs 48 to 72 hours to cure before anyone uses it, which we confirm on the day. You are normally back to using the bath two days after we arrive.
