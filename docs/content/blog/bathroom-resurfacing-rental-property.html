36.5% of bonds end with money going back to the landlord

NSW Fair Trading publishes every rental bond refund. We pulled the most recent quarter, April to June 2026, and counted: 72,939 refunds, of which 26,645, or 36.5%, had some amount paid to the agent or landlord rather than returned in full to the tenant. The median amount was $1,097. The median tenancy behind those refunds ran 601 days, a little under two years.

Being straight about what this does and does not show: the file records that money was retained, never why. Better than one in three tenancies ends in a dispute over condition worth about a thousand dollars, and the bathroom is the room most likely to look worse after two years than any other in the house.

Why a two-year cycle matters more than the price

Put the two together. In the suburbs where most of the stock is rented, the median tenancy is under two years, and better than a third of those tenancies end in money being withheld over condition. That is the real cycle a bathroom has to survive, and it is far shorter than the ten to fifteen years a coating is rated for.

It is also why we warrant resurfacing in a rental for 12 months rather than the 5 years an owner-occupier gets, and say so on the page rather than in the fine print. Tenanted bathrooms see harder, less predictable use and nobody inspects them between visits.

The vacancy maths, without a spreadsheet

You do not need exact numbers to see the shape of this decision. A full bathroom renovation takes a rental off the market for weeks while trades come and go in sequence. A surface refresh takes days, with one technician on site. The difference is rent the owner either collects or never sees.

A bathroom renovation is a sequence of trades: demolition, plumbing rough-in, waterproofing with its mandatory curing and inspection time, tiling, grouting, fit-off, and a cleaner at the end. Each trade has its own lead time, and each handover between them is a day or three where nothing happens. Even a smooth small renovation keeps a property off the market for weeks, and renovations in occupied-again timeframes are rarely smooth. All of that time is lost rent, on top of the works themselves.

Resurfacing inverts that. One technician, one or two days on site, no demolition, no waterproofing inspection, no skip bin on the driveway. The bathroom that was the reason the listing photos looked tired is presentable again inside the same week the old tenant handed back the keys.

The owner feels both halves of that, even if only one of them shows up as a line on a statement.

What fits inside a single vacancy window

A standard vacancy runs a week or two between tenancies once cleaning, inspection and minor repairs are accounted for. Resurfacing fits inside that gap because it is one trade rather than five, and nothing is demolished. Here is what we can realistically complete before the next tenant collects the keys:

  • Bath resurfacing. A worn, stained or chipped bath is stripped, prepped and recoated in a day, then needs roughly 24 hours to cure before use. Our bath resurfacing service is the single highest-impact item on this list, because the bath is usually the worst thing in the listing photos.
  • Wall and floor tile resurfacing. Dated tile colours recoated in white or a modern neutral, without removing a single tile.
  • Shower regrouting. Mouldy or crumbling grout raked out and replaced, which transforms how clean the shower reads at an open home.
  • Silicone replacement. Every stained bead cut out and rerun. Small job, outsized effect on inspection photos.
  • Vanity and basin work. Chip repairs and resurfacing for the other surfaces tenants notice first.

Combined, this is what our full bathroom makeover covers: the whole room refreshed as one booking, typically two to three days on site rather than weeks of sequenced trades.

NSW tenancy notes

Three practical points come up constantly in this work: whether surface work counts as a repair or a discretionary improvement, what notice applies if the property is still tenanted, and how the job affects an end of lease bond discussion. All of it is general information, not legal advice, and the rules change, so check current NSW Fair Trading guidance.

  • Repairs and maintenance while tenanted. Landlords have general obligations to keep rented premises in reasonable repair, and failed grout or a leaking shower can sit within that. Cosmetic-only resurfacing, on the other hand, is usually discretionary and easiest done vacant.
  • Access and notice. If work is done while a property is tenanted, proper entry notice applies, and as a practical matter the tenant needs to be able to avoid the resurfaced wet areas while coatings and silicone cure. A second bathroom makes tenanted work straightforward; a single-bathroom property usually means waiting for the vacancy window.
  • End of lease and the bond. Where bathroom damage is in dispute, distinguishing fair wear and tear from damage is a matter for the parties and, if needed, the tribunal. What we can contribute is speed and paperwork: a fast repair with dated before and after photos and an itemised invoice, so the file is clean whichever way the discussion goes.

When we tell the owner to replace instead

Resurfacing has limits, and pretending otherwise would cost you twice: once for our work, and again for the renovation the bathroom needed anyway. We flag these situations in the quote, from your photos where possible:

  • Drummy or loose tiles. If tiles are lifting off the wall or floor, coating them locks a failure in place. That is a tiling job, not a resurfacing job.
  • Failed waterproofing. Water staining on the ceiling below, swollen skirting outside the bathroom, or leaks that persist after grout and silicone are redone point to the membrane. No coating fixes that.
  • Rusted-through baths. Surface rust around a chip is repairable. A base rusted through from underneath is not, and that bath needs replacing.
  • Layout problems. If the bathroom is functionally wrong for the market, too small, no shower, inaccessible, new surfaces will not change what tenants say at the open home.

When we see any of these, the quote says so plainly, and we would rather lose the booking than resurface a bathroom that needed a builder.

Common questions from property managers

How long does a rental bathroom resurface take?

Most single bathroom resurfacing jobs are done in one day on site, with surfaces ready for use after the curing period, typically 24 hours for a bath. A full refresh of bath, wall tiles, regrouting and silicone usually fits inside two to three days, comfortably within a normal vacancy window.

Can work be done while the property is tenanted?

Yes, with the tenant's cooperation and correct entry notice. The main constraint is that resurfaced surfaces and fresh silicone cannot be used while curing, so a second bathroom helps. Many property managers simply schedule us for the vacancy window instead. Check current NSW Fair Trading guidance on entry and notice requirements.

Can you quote from photos?

Yes, that is our standard process. Send four to six photos of the bathroom, including close-ups of the damage, and we will send a free quote within 24 hours. Property managers can forward tenant photos or routine inspection photos; there is no need for an inspection visit before quoting most jobs.

What warranty applies for landlords?

The same per-material warranties as any customer: up to 5 years on resurfacing, 2 years on regrouting and 12 months on new silicone, each conditional on reasonable care of the surface. We supply the warranty and care instructions with the invoice so they can be kept on the property file.
