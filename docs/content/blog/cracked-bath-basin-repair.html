A chip is a surface problem. A crack usually is not. That difference decides whether a repair lasts five years or five weeks, and it is the reason two jobs that look similar in a photo are quoted and carried out completely differently. Here is what actually happens, on the bath and on the basin.

The short answer

Chips are filled, sanded, primed and refinished on the surface. Cracks in an acrylic bath need the base supported from underneath first, because the crack is a symptom of the floor flexing every time somebody steps in. Fill a crack without stopping the movement and it reopens. Basins skip the support stage.

What actually causes chips and cracks

Most damage traces back to one of four things, and which one you get depends less on bad luck than on what your bath is made of. A brittle coating over a rigid shell chips. A flexible shell cracks. Knowing which you have tells you most of what you need to know before anyone quotes it.

Something was dropped on it

This is the most common cause by a wide margin, and it is almost always a chip rather than a crack. A shampoo bottle, a phone, a razor, a shower head that has worked loose, a tool during a renovation. The damage concentrates where things land: the rim, the flat of the floor, and around the waste.

The base is flexing

The acrylic story, and the one that gets misdiagnosed most often. Nothing hit the bath. The floor moves under body weight because it is not properly supported underneath, and the acrylic eventually gives along the line of greatest movement. This is the cause that comes back if only the surface is repaired.

Thermal stress

Materials expand and contract with temperature, and they do not all do it at the same rate. Repeatedly filling a cold tub with very hot water, or pouring boiling water straight into it, puts localised stress into the surface. On its own it rarely cracks a bath outright, but it opens up damage that was already there.

Age and crazing

Fine spidery lines across the surface, usually on an older enamel bath or over an earlier coating that is breaking down. Crazing is the surface giving up rather than the bath failing, and it is one of the clearest signals that a full resurface will serve you better than a spot repair.

A previous coating is letting go

Different from crazing, and the two get mixed up constantly. Crazing is the original surface aging. This is somebody else's resurface failing: the coating lifts in patches and sheets, showing the older surface underneath in a different colour. It is the one case where the damage you can see is the least of it.

Patching a failing coating does not work, because the new material can only be as sound as what it is stuck to, and what it is stuck to is already letting go. The whole coating has to come back off, the surface underneath has to be prepared properly, and the bath is resurfaced from there. If a previous repair has reopened along the same line, this is usually why.

Telling the four kinds of damage apart

Before anyone can tell you what a repair involves, it helps to know which of these you are looking at. You can usually work it out in about a minute with a fingernail and a bit of pressure, and it is the same check we run from your photos.

Two things decide the answer, and most people only think about one of them. Depth matters: whether it moves, weeps, or catches a fingernail. So does length, and that is the one that surprises people. A repair has to disappear into the surface around it, and a hairline running right across a basin cannot be blended, so it takes a full resurface even though nothing about it is structural.

Why a cracked acrylic bath is not a surface problem

Acrylic baths are thin. They are made rigid by what sits under them, usually a bed of mortar, a timber cradle or a moulded base. When that support settles, was never packed properly, or has broken down, the floor of the tub starts to move under load. You can often feel it: stand in the bath and the base gives slightly underfoot.

That movement is what cracks the acrylic. The crack is the visible end of the problem, not the problem itself. It usually appears in the flattest part of the floor, near the middle or close to the waste, because that is where the flex is greatest and where a person's weight lands.

This is not our opinion about how baths should be installed. Decina, one of Australia's larger bath manufacturers, states it in their own installation instructions: "All acrylic baths need to be supported under all rims as well as supporting the full length of floor of the bath", and it is "a requirement of the water authorities that all non-self-supporting baths be supported the full length of the bath base on a mortar mix", with a minimum 50mm bed. A bath that flexes underfoot was very often not bedded properly when it went in.

How we repair a cracked acrylic bath

The sequence matters more than the materials, and it runs from the bottom up rather than the top down. Every stage exists to make the next one hold, which is why skipping the first stage is what makes a repair fail rather than merely look imperfect.

The stage nobody sees is the one that matters. Everything above the foam is cosmetic; the foam is what stops the crack coming back.

Cracks and chips in a basin: what changes

A basin is simpler, and it is worth knowing why rather than just being told the price differs. Nobody stands in a basin, so there is no load, no flex, and no need to support anything from underneath. The damage really is a surface problem, so the repair really is a surface repair.

The sequence is the same minus the first stage. High-strength filler in two to three coats, sanded flat between each, primed once smooth, then resurfaced to a gloss finish matching the rest of the basin. Same materials, same finish, considerably less work.

Why the DIY kit version usually fails

Most hardware-store repair kits give you filler and nothing else, which fixes the half of the problem you can see and ignores the half you cannot. On a basin that is often enough. On a bath floor it is a repair with a countdown on it, because the base is still moving underneath the new filler.

There is a second trap for anyone tempted to support the base themselves. Ordinary single-component expanding foam from a hardware store keeps expanding while it cures, and under a bath that pressure can lift and distort the floor of the tub, turning a crack into a hump. The two-part structural foam used for this work sets fast and to a controlled density instead.

When a crack cannot be repaired

We would rather say this before quoting than after. Some cracks are past repair, and putting a finish over them buys a few months at most. Being straight about it is cheaper for you than finding out later, and it is the reason we ask for photos of the whole bath rather than just the damage.

What it looks like afterwards, and how long it lasts

A repair done in this sequence is not meant to be visible. The filler is sanded flat before priming, and the resurfacing carries across the whole panel rather than stopping at the edge of the patch, so there is no ring or shadow where the repair sits. Colour is matched to the existing fixture, not to a standard white.

Resurfacing work carries a workmanship warranty of up to five years for owner-occupied homes, provided the surface is looked after as set out in our care instructions. Rentals are warranted for 12 months, and the reasons for the difference are set out on the warranty page. A supported base does not develop the same crack again, because the movement that caused it has gone.

Common questions about bath and basin cracks

Can a cracked bathtub actually be repaired?

Yes, in most cases. A crack in the floor of an acrylic bath is repaired by supporting the base from underneath, building the crack up with filler in layers, then priming and resurfacing the area to match. What cannot be repaired is a crack that passes right through the shell, or a base that still moves once supported.

Why did my bath crack in the first place?

Almost always because the base was not fully supported. Acrylic is thin and relies on what sits beneath it, so when that bedding settles or was never packed properly, the floor flexes under body weight. The crack appears where the flex is greatest, usually the middle of the floor or near the waste.

Is a basin crack repaired the same way?

The same materials and the same finish, but without the support stage. Nobody stands in a basin, so there is no flex to stop. The crack or chip is filled over two to three coats, sanded flat between each, primed once smooth, then resurfaced to a gloss finish that matches the rest of the basin.

My bath was resurfaced before and it is peeling. Can you patch it?

No, and patching it would fail faster than the original job. A coating is only as sound as what sits under it, and a peeling one is already separating from the bath. The old coating is stripped back, the surface underneath is prepared properly, and the bath is resurfaced from there. It is more work than a repair, and it is the only version that lasts.

Is a hairline crack a small job?

Not always, and length is what decides it rather than severity. A short hairline in one spot can be filled and blended. A hairline running right across a basin or a bath cannot be, because the repair would show as a line through the middle, so that one takes a full resurface even though the fixture is structurally fine.

Will the repair be visible afterwards?

It should not be. The filler is sanded flat before priming, and the resurfacing carries across the whole panel rather than stopping at the edge of the patch, so no ring or shadow forms where the repair sits. Colour is matched to your existing fixture rather than to a standard white.

Can I just use a repair kit from the hardware store?

On a basin chip, often yes. On a cracked bath floor it treats the symptom and leaves the cause, because the base keeps moving under the new filler. Single-component hardware-store foam is also the wrong product for supporting a base: it keeps expanding as it cures and can distort the floor of the tub.
