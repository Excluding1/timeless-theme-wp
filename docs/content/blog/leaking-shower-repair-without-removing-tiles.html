Most leaking showers are not leaking through the waterproofing membrane. Membrane failure does happen, but it is the exception. Here is how to tell the difference before anyone quotes you a rip-out.

How to tell where your shower is leaking

A shower has a handful of places water can escape, and they fail in different ways. Working out which one you have is mostly observation, and you can do a lot of it yourself before calling anyone.

Grout lines: the most common cause

Cement grout is porous and rigid. Buildings move, grout does not, so over the years it develops hairline cracks, then gaps, then crumbling sections. Water wicks through cracked grout slowly and constantly, every shower, every day. Run a fingernail along the lines in the wettest areas: grout that powders, flakes or has visible cracks is letting water past. If the grout is also permanently discoloured low on the walls, water is sitting inside it.

Silicone junctions

The joints where wall meets floor, where the two walls meet, and around the screen are sealed with silicone precisely because those junctions move too much for grout. Silicone hardens, shrinks and peels away with age. Look for beads that have pulled away from the tile, gone hard and cracked, or have visible gaps at the corners. These junctions carry more water than any grout line in the shower.

Shower screen seals

If water appears on the bathroom floor outside the screen but nothing shows up in the room below or on the other side of the wall, suspect the screen itself: perished door sweeps, gaps where the frame meets the tile, or failed silicone along the frame.

The drain

Leaks at the floor waste show up as damage directly below the drain. This one is a plumbing check rather than a surface repair, and it is worth ruling out early because the test is simple: block the drain, part-fill the base, and see whether the level holds.

The membrane: the exception

Under the tiles is a waterproofing membrane, and when it fails, water bypasses everything above it. This is the failure that genuinely requires tiles to come up. It is also, in our experience, the least common cause of a leaking shower, despite being the first thing many people fear and the first thing some companies quote for.

The no-tile-removal fix

When the leak is at the grout or silicone level, which is most of the time, the repair happens entirely from above the tiles. This is the core of our shower leak repair service, and done properly it has three parts:

  1. Rake out the failed grout, to depth. This is the step that separates a real repair from a cosmetic one. Skimming new grout over old cracked grout looks fine for a few weeks and then fails, because the cracked material underneath keeps moving. We remove the old grout to proper depth so the new grout has body.
  2. Regrout with epoxy. Epoxy grout is non-porous. Water cannot wick through it the way it wicks through cement grout, which is exactly the property a leaking shower needs. Our epoxy grout upgrade page covers the difference in detail.
  3. Cut out and replace the silicone. Every movement joint gets the old bead removed completely, the junction cleaned and dried, and new mould-inhibiting silicone run in its place.

One trade, one day on site, no demolition, no retiling, no waterproofing inspection, and the shower is back in service once everything cures. The full rake-out-and-regrout process is described on our shower regrouting service page.

When tiles do have to come up

Here is the honest section, because a company that fixes leaks from above has an obvious incentive to tell you every leak can be fixed from above. It cannot. Tiles have to come up when the membrane underneath has failed, and the warning signs are reasonably clear:

  • Drummy or lifting floor tiles. Tap the shower floor. A hollow, drummy sound means tiles have debonded, usually because water has been moving underneath them for a long time.
  • Damage that keeps spreading. Staining on the ceiling below, swollen skirting or bubbling paint on the far side of the wall that keeps growing even when the shower is rested.
  • A leak that survives a proper regrout. If the grout and silicone have genuinely been redone, to depth, with the junctions resealed, and water still gets through, the path is below the tiles.
  • Age and history. Showers from eras of poor waterproofing practice, or with a history of botched repairs, carry higher membrane risk.

Why acting early is the cheap option

A leaking shower never gets better on its own, and the damage it does is silent until it is not. Water tracking past failed grout ends up in the wall cavity and the subfloor, where it rots framing timbers, swells particleboard flooring, stains the ceiling below and feeds mould you cannot see. By the time a leak announces itself in the next room, the repair conversation is no longer about grout; it is about carpentry, plasterboard and repainting.

Caught early, at the cracked-grout stage, the fix is a one-day surface job. Left for a couple of years, the same leak can turn into building work involving several trades. The gap between those two outcomes is the strongest argument for dealing with a suspect shower this month rather than next year, and it is why we prioritise leak inspections in our scheduling.

Strata and apartment notes

In apartments the stakes are higher because the water usually goes somewhere that belongs to someone else: the ceiling of the unit below. A few general points if you are in strata (rules vary by scheme, so confirm with your strata manager):

  • Tell your strata manager early. Leaks that affect other lots or common property need to be on the record, and early notice keeps the conversation cooperative.
  • Responsibility commonly depends on where the failure is. Waterproofing membranes are often common property in NSW schemes, while grout and silicone inside your lot are commonly the owner's responsibility, but this depends on the scheme and its by-laws, so check rather than assume.
  • The practical upside: because grout-level and silicone-level leaks are within-lot surface repairs, they can usually be fixed quickly from above without the approvals, scaffolding and disruption that membrane works involve.

Prevention: keeping the next leak years away

Once a shower is watertight, a few habits keep it that way:

  • Treat silicone as a consumable. It lasts a few years in a busy shower, not forever. Replacing it when it starts to pull away is far easier than repairing what a failed bead lets through.
  • Watch the grout in the wettest corners each time you clean. Hairline cracks are the earliest and cheapest warning you will get.
  • Ventilate and squeegee. A shower that dries quickly is gentler on grout, silicone and everything behind them.
  • Do not ignore small changes: a new damp smell, a tile that suddenly sounds hollow, a skirting board starting to swell. Each is the shower asking for attention early.

Common questions about leaking shower repairs

Can a leaking shower be fixed without removing tiles?

In most cases, yes. The majority of shower leaks start at failed grout lines, perished silicone or screen seals, all of which can be repaired from above the tiles. Tiles only need to come up when the waterproofing membrane underneath has failed, which is the exception rather than the rule.

How do I know if my shower membrane has failed?

Warning signs include drummy or lifting floor tiles, water staining on the ceiling below or the other side of the wall, damp skirting boards outside the bathroom, and leaks that continue after grout and silicone have been properly redone. Membrane failure needs tile removal and rewaterproofing, and we say so upfront if we see it.

How long does a shower leak repair take?

A typical no-tile-removal repair, raking out failed grout, regrouting with epoxy and replacing the silicone, takes one day on site. The shower then needs to stay dry while everything cures, usually 24 to 72 hours depending on the products used. We confirm the exact timing in your quote.

Does regrouting fix a leaking shower?

Yes, when the leak is coming through the grout lines, which is the most common cause we see. Proper regrouting means raking the old grout out to depth, not skimming over it, then regrouting with epoxy and resealing the junctions. If the membrane has failed, regrouting alone will not fix it.
